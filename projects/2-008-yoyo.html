MIT 2.008 · Design & Manufacturing II

Injection-Molded Yo-Yo

A semester-long, five-person design and manufacturing project culminating in a production run of more than 50 functional yo-yos. I owned the main injection-molded body from concept through CAD, CAM, mold machining, molding, and final production.

01 · Design Ownership

From concept to a moldable, manufacturable body

The yo-yo depicts a dragon chasing a group of peasants—or perhaps the peasants are chasing the dragon—across a rocky landscape. My primary responsibility was the black main body, which forms the structural foundation of the assembly and interfaces with the decorative components and hardware.

Assembly architecture

The finished yo-yo combined injection-molded, thermoformed, and punched plastic parts. The main body had to support the outer decorative assembly while also accommodating the axle hardware and the press-fit interface used in the final design.

Exploded view of the five main yo-yo components.

Designed for repeatable production

The goal was not a single prototype. The design needed to be manufactured repeatedly, assembled consistently, and survive handling and drop testing across a run of more than 50 units.

Completed units from the production run.
02 · Tooling & Manufacturing

I carried the main body through CAD, CAM, mold machining, and injection molding.

After developing the part geometry, I generated the CAM toolpaths and machined the aluminum tooling used to produce the body. I then helped set up and operate the injection molding machine during process development and production.

Machined tooling The aluminum molds shown here were used to produce the injection-molded parts for the final assembly.
Production, not just prototyping The tooling and process had to be reliable enough to support dozens of completed assemblies rather than a one-off part.
03 · Process Development

Dialing in the injection molding process

Early molding runs revealed the tradeoff between incomplete filling and defects caused by overly aggressive settings. I helped tune the machine until the process produced consistent, fully formed parts.

04 · DFM Iteration

A manufacturing constraint forced a mid-project redesign

We initially treated the mold blanks as the limiting factor on product size. In practice, the available punch for the clear plastic components was more restrictive. By the time we discovered the issue, some tooling had already been manufactured.

Redesigning around the process

I helped scale down and simplify the yo-yo so it could be manufactured using the equipment actually available to us. The revised architecture reduced the number of press-fit interfaces and allowed the punched clear disk to retain multiple components in the assembly.

The experience made the central lesson of the course very tangible: manufacturing constraints need to shape the design from the beginning, before time and material are committed to tooling.

05 · Validation

Assembly, drop testing, and final production

I participated in final assembly and drop testing to identify weak points in the completed yo-yos. Testing informed assembly decisions and helped confirm that the revised design could survive normal handling.
