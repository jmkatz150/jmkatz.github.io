Axial Excellence Precision Lathe

A semester-long precision machine design project in which our six-person team designed, analyzed, manufactured, assembled, aligned, and validated a compact manual lathe for machining aluminum chess pieces. I contributed across the machine, with extensive involvement in CAD, detailed design, manufacturing, assembly, and testing.

01 · System Architecture

A complete machine, not a single mechanism

The project required the spindle, X cross-slide, Z carriage, structure, drive system, toolpost, alignment features, and operator controls to work as one precision system. The design was driven by measurable functional requirements for accuracy, repeatability, stiffness, cutting capacity, thermal behavior, actuation torque, durability, and manufacturability.

Precision motion and adjustability

The X-axis assembly combined dovetail guidance, a lead screw, gib preload, a custom indicator, anti-backlash features, way protection, and rotational adjustment. User-accessible alignment was intentionally built into the machine so manufacturing error could be corrected during setup and maintenance.

Final cross-slide CAD.

Spindle stiffness, preload, and thermal growth

The spindle design balanced shaft bending, bearing stiffness, preload, friction, fits, thermal expansion, speed, torque, and life. Analysis and testing were used together rather than treating the CAD model as the final answer.

Section view of the spindle assembly.
02 · Analysis-Driven Design

Model the physics, then use the model to make decisions

We used hand calculations, MATLAB models, error budgets, stiffness models, thermal models, bearing-life calculations, failure analysis, and state-point optimization to guide dimensions and architecture before committing to hardware.

Cutting-state optimization Feed rate, spindle speed, depth of cut, machining time, and motor power were evaluated together to identify useful operating points instead of selecting cutting parameters independently.
Design closed around measurable FRs Stiffness and error budgets established allowable deflections and misalignments; later measurements were used to check whether the real machine behaved like the model.
03 · CAD to Hardware

Extensive CAD was followed by extensive manufacturing

I worked throughout the machine rather than on a single isolated part. The semester involved continual detailed CAD, drawing and tolerance decisions, machining, fit-up, assembly, troubleshooting, and rework as modeled components became physical hardware.

1

Detailed CAD

Developed and revised machine geometry around functional requirements, interfaces, fits, preload, alignment, and available manufacturing processes.

2

Manufacture

Machined and fabricated custom components, then checked dimensions and fit as assemblies came together.

3

Assemble & debug

Real hardware exposed issues that models alone could not—friction, preload, misalignment, interference, and part-to-part variability.

4

Measure & iterate

Dial indicators, force measurements, CMM data, cutting tests, and repeated assembly adjustments drove final tuning.

Spindle manufacturing

Spindle friction was reduced dramatically after re-turning the shaft and running in the bearings, demonstrating how manufacturing and assembly details directly affected system performance.

Design for assembly and service

The cross-slide incorporated alignment pins, preload elements, accessible adjustment, custom indication, and protection for the ways—features intended to make precision achievable outside a perfect manufacturing environment.

04 · Hands-On Build

Design decisions were continuously checked against real hardware

The build phase involved much more than assembling finished parts. We repeatedly fit, adjusted, measured, reworked, and integrated components as the machine came together. I was heavily involved in that loop, moving back and forth between CAD, the machine shop, and the bench as problems surfaced.

Assembly and integration

Final performance depended on getting many mechanical interfaces right at once: bearing preload, shaft alignment, slide motion, structural stiffness, motor mounting, and the relative position of the cutting tool and spindle.

Hands-on assembly and adjustment during machine integration.

Subsystems before final integration

The machine was built and validated in stages. Subassemblies were checked independently before integration, making it easier to isolate friction, fit, alignment, and manufacturing issues before they became system-level problems.

Mechanical subsystems during assembly.
05 · Validation & Optimization

The machine was measured, cut with, dropped, and tuned

Testing was treated as part of the design process. Early smoke-test results exposed accuracy and repeatability problems; the team then adjusted rail angle and rotational alignment, reduced taper, and re-ran measurements until the machine met its final requirements.

From 0.3° taper to 0.07°

After the smoke test, the team used a dial indicator and the lathe’s built-in adjustment mechanisms to tune the Z-axis rail angle and cross-slide rotation. CMM verification showed the taper reduced from roughly 0.3° to 0.07°, while final accuracy and repeatability moved inside the project requirements.

06 · What I Took Away

Precision comes from the whole chain

The project made it clear that precision is not created by one tight tolerance. It comes from architecture, constraint, stiffness, thermal behavior, manufacturing, assembly, adjustment, measurement, and iteration working together. The most useful design feature is often not eliminating every error—it is making the important errors measurable and adjustable.
